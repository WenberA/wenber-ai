The honest starting point

"AI can write code faster than I can. So why am I spending months learning to do this myself?" This is the single most common question students bring to us at Wenber — and it deserves a real answer, not a slogan.

The fear is not irrational. AI tools now write a real share of production code, and executives moved fast: if a machine can generate a function, why pay a person to type it? Between 2023 and 2026, that logic drove some of the largest layoffs the tech industry has ever seen.

But logic that looks airtight on a slide doesn't always survive contact with production software. What happened next is the part most students never hear.

First, the fundamentals

What actually is AI, and how does it work?

Before deciding whether it threatens your future, it helps to know what it actually does. AI isn't thinking — it's pattern-matching at enormous scale.

Step 1

Data

Millions of examples — code, books, images, conversations — are fed into the model as raw training material.

→
Step 2

Training

The model adjusts billions of internal parameters until it can reliably predict patterns hidden in that data.

→
Step 3

Output

Given a prompt, it generates the statistically most likely response — a fast guess, not a verified fact.

What people actually use it for

Everyday assistants

Chatbots, voice assistants, and customer support that answer questions instantly.

Code & development

Autocomplete, bug detection, and generating first drafts of functions or features.

Health & diagnostics

Reading medical scans, flagging anomalies, and speeding up early drug research.

Finance & fraud detection

Spotting unusual transactions in real time across banks and payment systems.

Translation & language

Real-time translation and transcription across dozens of languages.

Recommendations

Deciding what you see next on social feeds, shopping sites, and streaming apps.

What happened next — the reversal

Now the same companies are quietly hiring the people back

By 2026, a pattern nobody predicted in 2023 became impossible to ignore: "boomerang hiring."

The plan in 2023

  • Cut engineering headcountAI can generate functions, features, even whole prototypes — so fewer people should be needed to ship software.
  • Replace support roles with botsKlarna's CEO publicly stated AI had replaced 700 customer service employees.
  • Reward the marketStock prices often rose on the announcement of AI-driven layoffs.

The reality by 2026

  • Quiet rehiring at scaleGartner projects ~50% of companies that cut jobs for AI reasons will rehire for similar roles by 2027.
  • Public regretForrester's 2026 Future of Work report found 55% of employers regret laying off workers for AI-related reasons.
  • Klarna reversed itselfThe same CEO quietly began rehiring human support staff in 2025.

Here's the mechanism behind that view: today's senior engineers were yesterday's juniors. If companies stop hiring and training juniors now, there's a real risk of a shortage of experienced engineers five to ten years from now — a problem some companies are already starting to worry about out loud.

What this means for you: the competition for a first job is real. The response isn't to avoid the field — it's to graduate with something AI-generated resumes can't fake: a working project you built and can explain line by line, and the judgment to know when code is actually correct.

What AI still can't do for you

Someone still has to design the system AI writes code inside of

AI is genuinely good at generating one function, one file, one small feature. It's far weaker at deciding how a thousand services should talk to each other, scale under load, and survive when one part fails. That gap is exactly where system architects and infrastructure engineers live.

  • Multi-file, cross-system reasoning is still the hard partBenchmarks consistently show AI models score far lower on real, multi-file engineering workflows than on isolated coding puzzles.
  • Microservices architecture is projected to triple in market sizebetween 2020 and 2026, as companies break monolithic apps into independent, scalable services — a design decision no AI makes for you.
  • Go (Golang) has become the backbone of cloud-native infrastructureDocker, Kubernetes, Terraform, and Prometheus are all written in Go — the tools that make modern distributed systems possible.
  • Major companies build on it for exactly this reasonUber, Netflix, and American Express rely on Go for high-concurrency, performance-sensitive backend systems.

Closer to home

What this means for Ethiopia

Every global trend above lands differently depending on where you're standing. Here's the honest picture for Ethiopia specifically.

The fear for Ethiopia

  • We risk becoming consumers, not buildersMost global AI models handle Amharic, Oromifa, Tigrinya, and Somali poorly, so imported AI tools serve Ethiopian users worse than they serve everyone else.
  • A real skills gap90% of Ethiopian hiring managers report struggling to find qualified AI and tech talent — the pipeline hasn't caught up to demand.
  • Talent drainSkilled developers who train locally are increasingly hired remotely by foreign companies, which is good income but doesn't always build Ethiopian products.

The opportunity for Ethiopia

  • A fast-growing marketEthiopia's IT services sector is valued around $3.5 billion and growing at roughly 11% a year.
  • Government-backed infrastructureThe Digital Ethiopia initiative and a $1 billion AI infrastructure partnership with NVIDIA are being built to grow local AI capacity.
  • The largest on-ramp in the country's historyOver 1.8 million Ethiopians are already enrolled in the national Ethio Coders program.
  • Real local demandTelecom and fintech leaders like Safaricom Ethiopia and Ethio Telecom are investing hundreds of millions in AI, actively hiring engineers who understand both AI and Ethiopian context.

The gap between these two futures isn't decided by AI. It's decided by how many Ethiopians learn to build with it instead of only consuming it.
